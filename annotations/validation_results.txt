Validation results. To assess the accuracy of the individual-to-polity linkage, we manually evaluated a stratified random sample of 284 matched records spanning both matching methods (polygon and URL), all three location origins (nationality, birth city, death city), five historical eras, and three notability tiers. Overall accuracy reached 84.2% (239/284; 95% Wilson CI: 79.5–88.0%).

Wikipedia URL matching proved more reliable than geospatial polygon matching (90.6% vs. 82.3%), with nationality-based URL matching achieving perfect accuracy (100.0%, n = 29). This result is expected: the Wikipedia URL of a Wikidata nationality entity typically refers directly to a historical polity — e.g., "Ottoman Empire" or "Qing dynasty" — and thus provides an unambiguous one-to-one link to the corresponding Cliopatria entry. By contrast, polygon matching must resolve spatial ambiguities (overlapping polities, imprecise coordinates) that introduce error.

Among location origins, death city matching exhibited the lowest accuracy (76.4%), compared with birth city (87.5%) and nationality (85.2%). Death cities are more prone to mismatch because prominent individuals frequently died far from where they were politically active: Suleiman the Magnificent (53 Wikipedia identifiers) died at Szigetvár in the Kingdom of Hungary — and was consequently matched to that polity rather than to the Ottoman Empire he ruled; similarly, Armando Calderón Sol, president of El Salvador, was matched to the United States because he died in Houston.

Accuracy varied across historical eras, with the Early Modern period achieving the highest rate (90.0%, n = 50), followed by Modern (87.5%) and Contemporary (87.2%). Ancient (76.8%) and Medieval (77.3%) periods showed lower accuracy, reflecting the greater uncertainty in both geographic coordinates and political boundaries for earlier centuries.

Counter-intuitively, individuals with low notability (fewer than 4 Wikipedia identifiers) were matched more accurately (87.5%) than those with high notability (77.9%). High-notability individuals tend to have well-documented but geographically dispersed careers that span multiple polities, making single-polity assignment inherently ambiguous. For instance, Peter the Iberian (23 identifiers) was born in the Kingdom of Iberia but was active mainly in the Eastern Roman Empire; Marwan I (12 identifiers) was born under the Rashidun Caliphate but ruled the Umayyad Caliphate; the writer Daniel Speer (29 identifiers) was born in Habsburg Wrocław but spent his career in Germany; and the Korean writer Park Tae-won (18 identifiers) was active in the Empire of Japan before moving to North Korea. Low-notability individuals, by contrast, typically lived more geographically circumscribed lives, making their polity assignment less ambiguous.
